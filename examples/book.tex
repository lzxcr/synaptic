\chapter{Preface}
Mathematical writing is read at several speeds. A reader may scan the page for
structure, pause at a definition, or follow a proof line by line. The page
should support all three behaviours without changing its visual grammar.

This example treats whitespace as structure: rules terminate a region, nodes
mark transitions, and quiet surfaces collect statements that belong together.

\section{Reading relationships}
Book content begins with a simple principle: decoration should reveal a
relationship already present in the argument. Repetition then turns that
relationship into navigation.

\begin{syndefinition}
  A page has \emph{visual continuity} when related components preserve their
  alignment, spacing, colour role, and typographic voice across contexts.
\end{syndefinition}

\begin{syntheorem}[Hierarchy principle]
  If every level of a document has one dominant cue and one supporting cue,
  then the levels remain distinguishable under both colour and monochrome
  delivery.
\end{syntheorem}
\begin{synproof}
  The dominant cue determines the first classification. The independent
  supporting cue resolves any collision remaining after colour is removed.
\end{synproof}

\section{Rhythm and measure}
Long-form pages need enough room for equations without producing tiring lines.
Mirrored inner and outer margins also need to leave space for binding and for
the reader's hands. Synaptic therefore treats measure and density as separate
controls rather than one generic scale.

\chapter{Semantic Emphasis}
\SynapticChapterSubtitle{Cards as part of the argument}
Definitions, theorems, examples, and remarks carry distinct semantic roles.
Their surfaces should be visible during scanning and quiet during sustained
reading.
\begin{synremark}
  A card is not a poster. Its border and fill should remain subordinate to the
  text it protects.
\end{synremark}
